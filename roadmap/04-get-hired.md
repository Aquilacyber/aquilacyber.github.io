# Phase 4: Get hired

**Time:** ongoing. Start in week 20 or earlier, while you are still finishing phase 3.

**Goal:** turn what you have learned into interviews.

Nobody can promise you a job. What you can control is how much evidence you show that you can do the work.

## 1. Build a portfolio

A portfolio is a public GitHub repository, or a small site, with three things in it:

- Your write-ups from phase 2.
- Your two track projects from phase 3, each with a README.
- A short "about" page that says what you are looking for.

Keep it tidy. Five good pieces beat thirty unfinished ones. Never publish anything that identifies a real victim, contains real credentials or breaks a platform's rules.

## 2. Write a one-page CV

- Put your projects above your education if you have no work history in security.
- For each project, give one line on what you did and one on the result. "Built a Python script that detects SSH brute force attempts in auth logs and tested it against 5,000 simulated events" says more than "interested in cybersecurity".
- Link to your portfolio.
- Leave out the list of every tool you have opened.

## 3. Pick one certification, if you pick any

A certification is useful when an employer asks for it or when it makes you finish a syllabus. Read the [certifications guide](../guides/certifications.md), look at ten job adverts you would apply for and buy the one they mention most. Exams are priced in US dollars, so check the current cost in Naira before you commit.

## 4. Know the entry points

Few people start as a penetration tester. Common first roles:

- SOC analyst or junior security analyst
- IT support or system administration with a move into security after a year or two
- GRC, compliance or IT audit analyst
- Junior application security or vulnerability management analyst
- Data protection officer support roles at organisations preparing for the NDPA

An IT support job that pays you while you learn is a perfectly good route. Many working security professionals started there.

## 5. Look in the right places

- Company career pages for banks, fintechs, telecoms and consulting and audit firms. The [Nigeria guide](../guides/nigeria.md) explains why.
- LinkedIn, with your profile set up to show your portfolio.
- Referrals. Tell people in the AquilaCyber community what you are looking for and what you can show.
- Volunteer and internship work. Helping a small business or a student body secure its accounts is real experience. Get permission in writing before you test anything.

## 6. Prepare for interviews

You will be asked about your projects first. Be ready to explain every step and every choice, including what you got wrong. After that, expect:

- Networking basics: DNS, the TCP handshake, the difference between TCP and UDP.
- A scenario: "You see a user logged in from two countries within an hour. What do you do?"
- For GRC: the difference between a policy, a standard and a procedure, and how you would run a risk assessment.
- For red team: how you would test a login page, and how you would write up what you find.

Practise answering aloud. Do a mock interview with someone from the community.

## 7. Keep learning while you apply

Apply steadily and keep a small learning routine going: one write-up or one new lab a week. Rejections are normal and early ones tell you very little. If three interviews in a row end at the same question, learn that topic.

## Checkpoint

You are doing this phase well when:

- [ ] Your portfolio is public and has at least five pieces in it.
- [ ] Your CV is one page and links to it.
- [ ] You send applications every week.
- [ ] You have done at least one mock interview.

## After your first role

Stay for at least a year if you can. Learn what the work is really like, then decide on a specialism using the [career profiles](../careers/README.md). Come back to the community and help the next person through phase 1.
